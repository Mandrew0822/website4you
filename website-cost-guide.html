How much does a website cost in 2026?

Website pricing is opaque by design. This guide breaks down what you are actually paying for, what different options cost, and how to spot a quote that will cause problems later.

The short answer

A website can cost anywhere from $0 to over $50,000, which is a useless range on its own. For a small, custom website with real design work, a sensible planning range in 2026 is $500 to $5,000. A focused one-page site often lands between $300 and $1,500. A multi-page small business site typically lands between $1,000 and $5,000. Full agency builds routinely start at $8,000 and climb quickly.

Our own prices sit at the lean end: $100 for a one-page site and $200 for a multi-page business site. We can do that because we build focused static websites, keep the process simple, and do not carry agency overhead.

The real question

Do not ask only “what does a website cost?” Ask “what will this website need to do, and what is a fair price for that outcome?”

What actually drives the price

Most quotes come down to seven variables:

  • Number of pages. A five-page site is not five times a one-page site, but each extra page adds design, content, and testing work.
  • Custom design vs template. A tailored layout costs more upfront and usually looks and performs better than a theme with your logo on it.
  • Content and copywriting. If you supply finished text and images, the price drops. If the studio has to research, write, and source, it rises.
  • Functionality. Contact forms are basic. Booking systems, stores, member areas, and custom integrations are not.
  • SEO and technical setup. Clean markup, metadata, structured data, redirects, analytics, and a sitemap take real time.
  • Timeline. Rush work costs more because it displaces other projects.
  • Revisions and process. More rounds, more meetings, and more stakeholders all add cost.

A good quote makes these variables explicit. A bad quote gives you one number and leaves the boundaries vague.

The ongoing costs nobody mentions

The build price is not the whole cost of owning a website. Plan for these:

  • Domain: roughly $10–$20 per year for a normal .com or local domain.
  • Hosting: often $0–$15 per month for a static site; more for WordPress or stores.
  • Business email: optional, but a custom address looks more credible than a free inbox.
  • Imagery: professional photos or licensed stock can add a few hundred dollars.
  • Updates: content changes, new services, and security maintenance are either your time or someone else’s.
  • Paid tools: booking systems, email platforms, and analytics usually have their own subscriptions.

Ask every provider who owns the domain and hosting account. If the answer is “we do,” ask what happens if you leave. You want control.

Templates vs custom vs agency

Templates

Templates are fast and inexpensive. They are a reasonable choice when the site is simple, the budget is genuinely tight, and you are comfortable doing the setup yourself. The trade-off is sameness: your site will share its structure and visual language with thousands of others.

Custom from a small studio

A custom site is designed around your content and customers. You get a better fit, cleaner code, and usually better performance. The trade-off is a higher upfront cost than a template — though often less than people expect when the scope is focused.

Agencies

Agencies make sense for large sites, multiple stakeholders, brand systems, or projects that need strategy, design, development, and content teams. You pay for coordination as well as craft. If you are a five-person business with a five-page site, you may be paying for layers you do not need.

How to budget sensibly

  1. Define the job of the site. Is it credibility, enquiries, bookings, sales, or a portfolio? One primary goal keeps scope honest.
  2. List the pages you truly need. Start with the minimum that serves customers. Add pages later if they earn their place.
  3. Prepare content early. This is the biggest controllable cost. Notes, photos, and examples reduce billable time.
  4. Separate build and ongoing costs. Budget for the domain, hosting, and updates from day one.
  5. Get scope in writing. Page count, revisions, integrations, launch support, and ownership should all be listed.

Red flags in a quote

  • A suspiciously low price with no scope, timeline, or contract
  • A guarantee of “number one on Google” — nobody can honestly promise that
  • Domain or hosting registered in the provider’s name only
  • Unlimited revisions with no definition of a revision
  • No explanation of who writes the content or supplies images
  • A large non-refundable deposit before you have seen anything in writing

Questions, answered

Is it cheaper to build a website myself?

A website builder can be cheaper in cash but costs more in time. You still pay for hosting, a domain, and often premium features, and you are responsible for design, copy, SEO, and maintenance. A custom site is usually worth it when your time is limited or the website needs to win customers.

Why do website quotes vary so much?

Quotes vary because scope, process, and experience vary. A quote should state the number of pages, what content is included, how many revisions, what integrations are covered, and who handles launch. Comparing those details matters more than comparing headline prices.

What are the ongoing costs of a website?

Expect a domain (roughly $10–20 per year), hosting (often $0–15 per month for a static site), and optional costs such as business email, stock imagery, or ongoing updates. A custom static site has no mandatory plugin subscriptions.

Do I need to pay the full amount upfront?

With website4you, 50% is paid to start and 50% at handover. The full scope and price are confirmed in writing first. Avoid any provider who asks for the full amount with no written agreement.
