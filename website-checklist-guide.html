What to include on your website: a 12-point checklist.

Most websites do not fail because they lack features. They fail because visitors cannot tell what the business does, why they should trust it, or what to do next. This checklist covers the essentials — in the order they matter.

Published November 7, 2026 7 min read By website4you
On this page
  1. The four essentials
  2. Trust and clarity
  3. Technical foundations
  4. Questions, answered

The four essentials

1. A clear one-line explanation

A visitor should understand what you do, who it is for, and where you are (if relevant) within a few seconds. Write it plainly. “Family-run plumbing serving North Leeds” beats “Engineering comfort for the modern home.”

Put this at the top of the homepage, in the page title, and in your social descriptions. If you cannot explain it simply, the rest of the site will struggle.

2. One primary action

Every page should have an obvious next step: contact you, book a call, request a quote, buy, or join a list. One primary action, repeated at natural points, beats a row of five equal buttons that make people choose nothing.

3. Contact details that are easy to find

Include an email address, a phone number if you want calls, and any relevant location or service area. Put contact in the header, footer, and at the end of key pages. Make the email a real link, not an image, so it works on a phone.

4. Proof

Proof lowers risk. It can be testimonials, reviews, project examples, before-and-after work, credentials, client logos, or a clear explanation of how you work. A short, specific testimonial with a name is worth more than a wall of five-star icons.

Trust and clarity

5. Services described by outcome

Do not just list what you offer. Explain what changes for the customer. “Kitchen fitting” is a service. “A finished kitchen, fitted in ten days with one point of contact” is a reason to call.

6. A real about section

People want to know who they are dealing with. A photo, a short story, and a clear description of how you work build more trust than a paragraph of corporate language. If you are a one-person business, say so — it is often an advantage.

7. A price signal

You do not have to publish every price. But some indication — “projects from $200,” “typical engagement $2–5k,” or “free quote” — filters out mismatched enquiries and makes serious buyers more comfortable.

8. An FAQ that handles objections

Answer the questions you get on the phone: how long it takes, what is included, what happens if something changes, and what the customer needs to prepare. Good FAQ copy reduces support time and helps search visibility.

Technical foundations

9. A mobile-first layout

More than half of typical small business traffic arrives on a phone. Design for the small screen first: readable text, tap-friendly buttons, no horizontal scrolling, and no giant images that push the point below the fold.

10. Fast pages and optimized images

Speed affects both conversion and search visibility. Compress images, size them correctly, use modern formats where possible, and avoid loading scripts you do not need. A fast site feels more professional before a visitor can explain why.

11. SEO foundations

Give every page a unique title and description, one clear H1, a logical heading structure, descriptive link text, and a sitemap. Add structured data that describes your business, services, and FAQs. These are the basics; content and reputation do the rest.

12. Analytics, ownership, and a way to update it

Before launch, make sure you can see how the site performs and that you control the domain, hosting, and files. A site you cannot measure or move is a liability. Set up basic analytics, keep a record of logins, and decide how content changes will be handled.

The quick test

Show your homepage to someone unfamiliar with your business for ten seconds. Ask them what you do, who it is for, and what they would do next. If they hesitate, fix the top of the page first.

Questions, answered

How many pages does a small business website need?

Most small businesses need a homepage, a services page, an about page, and a contact page. That can be four pages, or it can be one well-structured page. Add pages when they serve a clear customer question or search intent, not to hit a number.

Do I need a blog?

Only if you will maintain it and it serves a purpose. A useful guide that answers a real customer question is worth more than twelve abandoned posts. If you do not want to write regularly, invest in stronger core pages instead.

What should go above the fold?

A clear statement of what you do, who it is for, and the next action. Add one piece of proof if it fits. Avoid a large decorative image that pushes the explanation below the screen.

Do I need a privacy policy?

If you collect personal information through forms, analytics, or cookies, you generally need a privacy policy that explains what you collect and why. Rules vary by location, so use a policy appropriate to your business and, where needed, get professional advice.
